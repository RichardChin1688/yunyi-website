Concept

“Nourishing” and “treating” are not the same thing

Once this line is clear, many questions about wellness answer themselves.

In Chinese medicine, treating addresses illness. It requires a practitioner with a licence, working in a proper institution, who examines the patient and gives a plan. Nourishing addresses daily life: food, rest, the seasons and mood.

The two are not alternatives but a division of labour. Nourishing cannot replace treatment, and treatment does not remove the need for everyday care. One idea often quoted is “treat before illness” — attending to care before anything goes wrong. That is where nourishing sits.

Which is why the most important boundary for anyone in wellness is: do not step over into diagnosis and treatment. A person can explain how to cook with the season; they should not tell anyone what is wrong with their body.

Yunyi's public content follows the same rule: prevention and everyday care here, diagnosis and treatment in hospital. Each does its own job.

Constitution

Constitution differs from person to person

The same act can feel entirely different to two people — that is why constitution matters.

Nine constitution types are traditionally described: balanced, qi-deficient, yang-deficient, yin-deficient, phlegm-damp, damp-heat, blood-stasis, qi-stagnant and special constitution. The purpose of this classification is to give people a shared way of describing how they feel. It is not a diagnosis.

Actual assessment requires a professional, in a proper setting, taking into account your current state, history and habits. Two people of the same type can differ a great deal in detail.

So the more useful habit is: understand yourself first, then choose what suits you. That is also why the brand holds to “one person, one formula — that is what is real”: say clearly that people differ, then talk about care.

NoteTypes

Nine commonly described types

  • Balanced: generally steady, adapts well
  • Qi-deficient: tires easily, short of breath, weak voice
  • Yang-deficient: feels the cold, cold hands and feet
  • Yin-deficient: dry mouth, warm palms and soles
  • Phlegm-damp: heaviness, drowsiness
  • Damp-heat: oily, bitter or sticky taste
  • Blood-stasis: duller complexion, fixed discomfort
  • Qi-stagnant: low mood, frequent sighing
  • Special constitution: allergic tendency
These are traditional descriptions for understanding, not diagnostic criteria, and not for drawing conclusions about yourself.

These three points are where “one person, one formula” comes from.

Season

Seasons and eating: follow the calendar

Follow the season and eat according to need — not one fixed method.

There are twenty-four solar terms in a year. In an agricultural society they were a timetable for farm work; in daily life they became a reference for eating and resting. The idea is simple: eat what the season grows, and change how you cook when the weather changes.

The Inner Canon contains a much-quoted line: “eat and drink in measure, keep regular hours, and do not overwork.” It is not about eating something special but about rhythm and proportion — much the same as what people today call regular hours and normal meals.

In practice, the two things easiest to keep up are eating a little less and moving a little more, and starting meals with vegetables. An old saying adds, “if you want a child to be well, keep them a little hungry and a little cool.” No secret recipes — only habits.

A food structure often mentioned is the “211 plate”: two parts vegetables, one part protein, one part whole grains. The advantage is that it needs no calculation — a glance at the plate is enough.

NoteSeasons

Rough direction by season

  • Spring: growth begins; lighter food, more seasonal vegetables
  • Summer: heat consumes fluids; watch hydration and moderation
  • Autumn: the air turns dry; food tends to be moistening
  • Winter: cold and storing; food warm and regular
Traditional general guidance for understanding, not directed at any individual.

  • Keep three meals roughly on time and hours roughly regular

Moxa

What moxibustion is

Knowing what a traditional method is and where it comes from is more useful than hearing what it is said to fix.

Moxibustion is one of the traditional external methods of Chinese medicine: warmth produced by burning moxa rolls or cones, made from wormwood, acts on the body surface. Wormwood is harvested, then usually aged; careful practice ages it for several years.

On the material, the founder of Yunyi describes a specific practice: “We contracted land — two plots. The wormwood is harvested at the Dragon Boat Festival, then left to purify with air moving through it, to ferment internally. The following year it is worked again, left another year, then branded and sealed. So ours is generally three years or more.”

Contracted land, harvest in season, ageing, reworking — the process is neither quick nor cheap. It shows care in the material, not a promise of effect.

To be clear: Yunyi treats its moxa products as everyday wellness goods, not medical use, and makes no efficacy claim. For a specific condition, consult a professional first.

Food

Polygonatum and the craft of “nine processes”

“Nine processes” describes a traditional craft, not an adjective for an effect.

Polygonatum is one of the ingredients traditionally used in nourishment and appears in herbal literature across the dynasties. “Nine processes” refers to the traditional craft of repeated steaming and drying, which changes the state and mouthfeel of the material. The classic formulation is “nine steamings, nine sunnings”; in practice the number varies.

The point of the craft is to handle the material, not to create an effect. That is a useful key to understanding traditional nourishment: look at how it is made before asking what it is.

Yunyi's nine-processed Polygonatum sits in the “organic nourishment” direction and is a food-based daily product — not a medicine, not for medical use, and not for diagnosing or treating disease. The related product line is being developed; availability will be announced through official channels.

Yunyi is a Chinese medicine wellness and organic food-based nourishment brand from Shenzhen. What is the difference between “Chinese medicine” and “herbal” wellness?

“Herbal” usually refers to plant-based ingredients. The materials used in Chinese medicine also include animal and mineral substances, so the two are not equivalent. Yunyi formulates and selects materials along the lines of Chinese medicine rather than simply using plants — which is why the brand describes itself as Chinese medicine wellness, not herbal wellness.

What does “organic nourishment” mean?

“Nourishment” (食养) means caring for yourself through everyday food; it is the most daily layer of the Chinese medicine idea of treating before illness. “Organic” refers to how the material is grown and sourced. Yunyi’s organic nourishment direction comes from the founder’s belief that everyday wellness starts in your own kitchen; the related product line is being developed.

What does “one person, one formula” mean?

It is a line the founder returns to: “One person, one formula — that is what is real. Everyone’s constitution is different, and the same formula does not suit everyone.” The brand uses it as its editorial stance: general knowledge and method only, no individual constitution assessment, and no single answer for everybody.
